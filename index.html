ANCHA RADHAKRISHNA MURTHY

He gave faces to presidents and prime ministers, actors and saints — then history forgot his own.

The story

The sculptor history forgot

In the 1950s, a young man from a farming village near Tenali carved his way into Madras studios, Delhi's corridors of power, and an international stage. Presidents sat for him. Actors were cast in bronze by his hands. Then, quietly, he went home to farm his field — and the world moved on.

This is his family putting his name back where it belongs.

01

The Runaway

Born in 1925 in Bodapadu village near Tenali — the only son of a farming family — he was shaping clay and drawing long before anyone taught him. At fifteen, his school spotted the gift and asked him to sculpt Gandhi, Nehru and Jesus for the school. He worked day and night. But his father needed him in the fields, so he ran away — to Macharla, where Adavi Bapiraju became his first guru and taught him painting, and painting only.

02

The Studios

First came study: a Government Diploma of Fine Arts in Chennai, passed with First Class — learning sculpture from Devi Prasad Roy Chowdhury of Bengal.

In 1952 he apprenticed under art director Madhavapeddi Gopalam at Vahini Studio, Madras — the dream factory of Telugu cinema. He rose to become an art director himself at Saradhi Studio.

In 1962, on the encouragement — protsaham — of journalist Narla Venkateswara Rao, a family friend who treated him like a younger brother, he moved to Hyderabad. Under Narla's blessings he sculpted prolifically.

03

The Exhibition

In October 1956, the Government Museum at Egmore hosted his one-man show — paintings, drawings and sculptures together. It was opened by Chief Justice P. V. Rajamannar. The catalogue billed him simply: A. R. KRISHNA.

Then came the invitation that could have changed everything: a government scholarship to study in Italy. He applied. He was ready. And then smallpox took his eldest daughter, and he could not leave his family in their grief. He let Italy go — and stayed with the people he loved.

1956 exhibition catalogue

04

The President

In 1962, his own students — by then professors at Andhra University — introduced him to President Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan. He was invited to Raj Bhavan. Three sittings, thirty minutes each — the President of India sat still while a farmer's son from Bodapadu shaped his likeness in clay. He was one of the few sculptors in Andhra of his time.

05

The Teacher

In 1964 he completed a postgraduate course in sculpture at the Madras School of Art under Devi Prasad Roy Chowdhury — whose teachers wanted to send him to America for further study.

Everything he had learned, he would one day pass on — to a student history does remember.

06

The Musician

He played the sitar. The same hands that shaped bronze drew music from strings — one more art folded into a life that refused to hold just one.

07

The Return

From 1978 to 1985 he lived in Bangalore, chasing an obsession: a flying bicycle. He built prototypes and ran experiments. When his health declined, he came home to Bodapadu and farmed his own field to the end — and it was there, in the village, that a young Katuri Venkateshwara Rao met him and became his student. He died on March 29, 2001. The man who sculpted a President ended his days as a farmer — not defeated, just home.

The student remembers the teacher — even if history does not.

08

The Rediscovery

In May 2017, the Telugu daily Sakshi profiled him as an “international artist” — one of the only published records of his life. Today his family is gathering every photograph, catalogue and memory into this archive, and a book on his life is in progress.

The forgetting ends here.

    Duggirala Gopala Krishnayya statue — sculpted in Chennai at Pragada Kotaiah's request, installed in Chirala.

    Bangalore — prototyping the aerocycle, a flying bicycle; experiments until his health declines.

    Returns to Bodapadu and farms his own field. There, Katuri Venkateshwara Rao meets him and becomes his student.
